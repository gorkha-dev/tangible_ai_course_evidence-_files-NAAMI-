When a native English speaker looks at Hangeul for the first time, it feels like alien geometry. The secret to Day 1 is breaking the illusion that it's a terrifying character-based system like Chinese and showing them it's actually an alphabet puzzle that can be cracked in a single afternoon.

Here is the exact, low-stress lesson plan for Day 1.

Day 1 Lesson Plan: Crack the Hangeul Code
1. The Mindset Shift (5 Minutes)
The Hook: Tell them to forget everything they know about reading Chinese characters or memorizing English spelling. Hangeul was engineered in 1443 by a king who wanted everyone to read. It's a phonemic alphabet—it literally diagrams what your mouth and tongue are doing.

2. Introduce the "Building Block" Secret (15 Minutes)
Explain that Korean blocks sounds into syllables. Every syllable block is like a mini-box containing Consonant + Vowel (and sometimes a final consonant called a batchim, which we ignore for today).

Show them the 4 basic vowels that look like lines and dashes:

I (ee sound, like in meet)

ㅏ (ah sound, like in father)

ㅜ (oo sound, like in boot)

ㅗ (oh sound, like in boat)

3. Match with 4 Easy Consonants (15 Minutes)
Teach 4 consonants that have direct English equivalents so they get an immediate win:

ㅁ = M

ㄴ = N

ㅅ = S

ㅇ = Silent placeholder when leading a syllable (or ng at the end).

4. The "Aha!" Moment: Combining Them (15 Minutes)
Show them how to snap the pieces together into actual words:

ㅇ+ㅏ=아 (A, like the "ah" sound when the doctor checks your throat).

ㄴ+ㅏ=나 (Na, meaning I / me).

ㅁ+ㅏ+ㄴ+ㅏ=만나 (Manna, part of the verb to meet—though keep it simple with just Na for today).

ㅅ+ㅏ+ㄹ+ㅏ+ㅁ=사람 (Saram, meaning person).

5. Three Survival Slang/Polite Words to Walk Away With (10 Minutes)
Don't force grammar yet. Give them three high-frequency words they can use immediately:

안녕하세요 (Annyeonghaseyo): Hello.

감사합니다 (Gamsahamnida): Thank you.

네 (Ne): Yes.

Homework / Next Steps

Day 1: Mouth Mechanics & The Vowel Map
Focus: Retraining the tongue and lips. English speakers pronounce vowels deep in the throat or wide across the lips. Korean vowels require precise, forward mouth shapes.

Key Lessons:

The "Flat" Vowels: Teach ㅓ (eo — sound made with lips neutral, tongue pulled slightly back, like the "u" in cut) vs. ㅏ (a — open jaw, forward tongue). English speakers mess this up constantly.

The Rounded Vowels: ㅗ (o — tight, rounded lips) vs. ㅜ (u — puckered lips like whistling).

Mouth Muscle Drills: Have them exaggerate the jaw drop for ㅏ and the tight circle for ㅜ for 5 minutes straight until their facial muscles feel it.

Day 2: The Sound Trap—Aspirated vs. Tense vs. Plain Consonants
Focus: This is the #1 hurdle for English speakers because English doesn't make these distinctions. Korean has three tiers of stops (ㅂ/ㅍ/ㅃ, ㄷ/ㅌ/ㄸ, ㅈ/ㅊ/ㅉ).

Key Lessons:

Plain (Relaxed): ㅂ,ㄷ,ㄱ (Sounds like a mix between B and P, or D and T. No puff of air).

Aspirated (Breathful): ㅍ,ㅌ,ㅋ (Heavy burst of air. Test it: hold a piece of paper in front of your mouth; it should fly away).

Tense (Constricted): ㅃ,ㄷ,ㄲ (Stiff throat, zero air puff, sharp snap).

The Tissue Test: Spend 30 minutes passing a tissue back and forth while saying 바 (drops), 파 (blows away), and 빠 (stays still).

Day 3: Sound Shifting & Flow (Batchim + Liaison)
Focus: Real Koreans don't speak like robots reading isolated dictionary words. Sounds morph when they crash into each other.

Key Lessons:

The Rule of Reslinking (Liaison): When a syllable ends in a consonant (batchim) and the next syllable starts with an empty ㅇ, the final consonant slides over to fill the empty slot.

Example: 국+어 becomes 구거 (gugeo, not guk-eo).

Naslization & Assimilation (Keep it light): Quick intro on how ㅂ turns into an m sound when it hits a nasal consonant.

Shadowing Practice: Play a native audio clip of a basic phrase 3 times. Have them mimic the exact pitch, rhythm, and flow—not just the individual letters.
